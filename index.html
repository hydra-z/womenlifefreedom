زن, زندگی, آزادی

Zan  ·  Zendegi  ·  Azadi

Woman —  Life  —  Freedom

A chant born from Kurdish resistance, carried into the streets of Iran in the autumn of 2022, and spoken again through a much larger uprising that began in December 2025. It is now heard in dozens of languages as a call for bodily autonomy, dignity, and the right to choose one's own life.

Latest — as of early 2026

A new, larger wave of unrest

Reporting below is drawn from human rights monitors, wire services, and reference sources and is summarized carefully because the situation remains contested, fast-moving, and difficult to verify independently amid internet restrictions inside Iran.

A separate, much larger protest wave began on 28 December 2025, initially triggered by economic collapse — a sharply devalued currency, high inflation, and shortages — rather than by a single incident like Amini's death. Demonstrations spread from markets and universities to cities nationwide, and protesters' demands broadened from economic relief to open calls against the ruling system.

Human rights organizations, including Amnesty International, report that Iranian security forces carried out a violent crackdown, especially on 8–9 January 2026, which multiple monitors describe as the deadliest period of state repression in the Islamic Republic's history. Casualty estimates vary widely by source and remain unverified given a near-total internet shutdown imposed by authorities from early January into late May 2026. On 11 February 2026, Iran's president publicly apologized for the killings.

The relationship between this newer uprising and the original 2022 "Woman, Life, Freedom" movement is itself debated: some activists and victims' families have publicly objected to the slogan being set aside by parts of the opposition, while others argue the 2025–26 protests represent a broader coalition than the 2022 movement alone. This page does not take a position on that debate — it aims only to describe it.

01 — Origin

A chant with two homelands

Long before it filled the streets of Tehran, Jin, Jiyan, Azadî — Kurdish for "Woman, Life, Freedom" — was sung by Kurdish women's movements across the Kurdish regions of Turkey, Syria, Iraq, and Iran, rooted in decades of organizing for women's political and social liberation.

In September 2022, the phrase entered a new chapter. Jina (Mahsa) Amini, a 22-year-old Kurdish-Iranian woman, died in the custody of Iran's morality police after being detained for allegedly wearing her hijab improperly. Her death, first mourned in her hometown of Saqez, ignited protests that spread to nearly every province in Iran within days.

Demonstrators — students, schoolgirls, shopkeepers, and eventually people well outside the country — adopted the Kurdish chant, translating it into Persian: زن، زندگی، آزادی. What began as a slogan of mourning became one of the broadest and most sustained protest movements in Iran's modern history, and a phrase now recognized far beyond the region that coined it.

"It was never only about a scarf. It was about who gets to decide."

— commonly cited framing among protest organizers, 2022

02 — The Three Words

What each word carries

زن

Woman Zan

A demand that women's autonomy over their own bodies, dress, and choices be recognized as a baseline right, not a negotiated privilege.

زندگی

Life Zendegi

A rejection of a politics organized around control and restriction, in favor of the right to an ordinary life — to work, study, dance, and move freely.

آزادی

Freedom Azadi

A broader call for civil liberties and political freedom that, for many demonstrators, extended well beyond dress codes to the structure of governance itself.

03 — Timeline

How it unfolded

A partial record. Dates and details of a fast-moving, contested period are drawn from widely reported public accounts and may be refined as more information emerges.

  1. ❀
    13 Sept 2022

    Detention

    Jina (Mahsa) Amini is detained by Iran's morality police in Tehran, reportedly over how she wore her headscarf.

  2. ❀
    16 Sept 2022

    Death in custody

    Amini dies in a Tehran hospital. Authorities attribute her death to pre-existing illness; her family and eyewitnesses dispute this account.

  3. ❀
    17 Sept 2022

    First protests

    Mourners at Amini's funeral in Saqez chant "Jin, Jiyan, Azadî." Protests begin spreading to Sanandaj, Tehran, and beyond within hours.

  4. ❀
    Late Sept 2022

    Nationwide spread

    Demonstrations, including acts such as public hair-cutting and hijab removal, spread to dozens of cities and universities across nearly every province.

  5. ❀
    Oct – Dec 2022

    Crackdown and global attention

    Rights monitors report mass arrests, internet restrictions, and significant casualties. Solidarity protests and vigils are held in cities worldwide.

  6. ❀
    2023 – 2025

    An ongoing conversation

    Public debate over mandatory hijab law, enforcement, and broader civil liberties continues inside Iran, alongside continued advocacy from the diaspora, against a backdrop of deepening economic crisis.

  7. ❀
    28 Dec 2025

    A new uprising begins

    Protests erupt in Tehran over economic collapse — currency devaluation, inflation, and shortages — and quickly spread nationwide, becoming the most significant unrest since 2022–23.

  8. ❀
    8–9 Jan 2026

    A violent crackdown

    Rights monitors report a mass crackdown with a large number of deaths — described by several observers as the deadliest period of repression in the Islamic Republic's history. A near-total internet shutdown begins soon after.

  9. ❀
    11 Feb 2026

    A public apology

    Iran's president, Masoud Pezeshkian, publicly apologizes to the nation for the killings during the crackdown.

  10. ❀
    2026 – ongoing

    An unresolved moment

    Debate continues — inside Iran and across its diaspora — over how this newer uprising relates to "Woman, Life, Freedom," what accountability should follow, and what comes next.

04 — Voices

Why a chant travels

Chants move because they compress a shared feeling into a shape anyone can carry. "Woman, Life, Freedom" has been sung in Persian, Kurdish, Turkish, Arabic, French, and English — outside embassies, at film festivals, in classrooms, and in songs written in its name. Its brevity is part of its reach: three words, each one doing a different kind of work, together forming a claim that is at once personal and political.

This site aims to document that phrase's path — where it came from, what it has meant to the people who chant it, and how it has been received elsewhere — rather than to speak for any single movement or organization.

05 — Around the World

A phrase that crossed borders

Streets and squares

Solidarity demonstrations were held in cities including Berlin, Paris, London, Toronto, Los Angeles, and Sydney through late 2022 and into 2023.

Culture

Musicians, filmmakers, and writers referenced the phrase in new work; it appeared on stages, in murals, and at award ceremonies.

Institutions

Human rights bodies and several governments issued statements referencing the protests and the treatment of demonstrators.

Ongoing debate

Observers continue to discuss the movement's long-term impact on policy, public opinion, and civic life inside Iran — an open and evolving question.

About Us

Who we are

We are Ramin Ebrahimi, Zheela Mahmoudi, and Zhala Mahmoudi, three Iranian Kurds from the Sorani Kurdish community.

Like many others, our lives changed because of our commitment to human dignity, freedom, and justice. We believe that every person deserves to live in safety, express their opinions freely, and enjoy equal rights without fear of persecution.

Due to the political situation in Iran and the risks we faced, we were forced to leave our homeland in search of safety. Our journey was not a choice but a necessity for survival.

Our Story

Leaving our country meant leaving behind our families, our homes, our memories, and everything familiar. We hoped to find protection and security, but instead we have faced many new challenges.

We are currently living in Peshawar, Pakistan, under extremely difficult circumstances. We have no stable legal status, no permanent source of income, and no guarantee of personal safety.

We have experienced repeated police harassment and live with the constant fear of detention or deportation. Returning to Iran would place our lives, freedom, and future at serious risk.

In addition, officers from Pakistan's security services have questioned us on several occasions, increasing our fear and uncertainty. Living under constant pressure has severely affected our mental well-being and our ability to build a normal life.

Despite these hardships, we continue to support one another and remain committed to the values we believe in.

Why We Need Protection

Our concerns are not only about our present situation in Pakistan but also about the serious dangers we may face if we are returned to Iran.

We fear persecution because of our Kurdish identity, our political background, and our support for the "Woman, Life, Freedom" movement. We believe that returning to Iran could expose us to serious human rights violations.

For this reason, we respectfully seek international protection and humanitarian assistance.

Our Hope

Although our journey has been painful, we have not lost hope.

We dream of living in a country where we can work honestly, continue our education, contribute to society, and live without fear.

We believe that every refugee deserves safety, dignity, and the opportunity to rebuild their life.

  • A safe and dignified future
